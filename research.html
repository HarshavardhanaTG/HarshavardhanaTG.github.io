Research

My research builds neuromotor interfaces that decode human intent directly from muscle activity, with applications spanning assistive communication, human-computer interaction, and robot learning. On the communication side, I develop non-invasive interfaces that restore speech for individuals who have lost the ability to speak following laryngectomy or radiation-induced damage to the speech articulators after cancer treatment; the same underlying technology extends to broader human-computer interaction, as seen in emerging products such as Meta's EMG wristband. I also study how humans coordinate highly dexterous tasks using multimodal measurements such as EMG (electromyography), eye gaze, and egocentric video, to translate principles of human sensorimotor coordination into more capable robots.

A central goal of my research is to make neuromotor interfaces robust and intuitive enough for real-world use. To this end, I study the underlying properties of neuromotor signals and develop algorithms that generalize across populations and operate efficiently on embedded devices in natural environments. My work takes a principled machine-learning approach, with foundational contributions to multivariate time-series learning for biosignals, speech and audio modeling, and multimodal models for interaction with the physical world. At UC Davis, I have conducted large-scale human-subject studies and built neuromotor interfaces end to end, spanning experimental design and data collection through algorithm development and deployment with real users.

EMG-Based Hand-Gesture Interfaces

>90 participants

This work marked my first investigation into EMG as a modality for human-computer interaction. EMG signals recorded at the wrist can enable interfaces for hand-gesture recognition, keyboard typing, and handwriting recognition. A fundamental challenge, however, is the substantial variability of these signals across individuals. In this project, I asked whether EMG possesses an inherent structure that could serve as an inductive bias for machine-learning algorithms. This question was motivated by the success of structured signal representations in other domains, particularly spectrotemporal representations that have been fundamental to speech and audio modeling.

To investigate this question, I conducted a large-scale study, the largest of its kind at the time, across a demographically diverse population that included individuals with high BMI and adults over 60 years. We found that when EMG signals are represented through covariance matrices, they exhibit a consistent geometric structure that is sufficiently informative to decode discrete gestures even without supervision. Remarkably, this structure persists across individuals and remains largely unaffected by factors such as subcutaneous fat, skin hydration, and age. This work established my foundational understanding of neuromuscular signals and provided the basis for my subsequent work on more complex EMG-based speech interfaces.
