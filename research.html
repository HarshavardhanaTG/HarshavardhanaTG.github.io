Research

I build non-invasive neuromotor interfaces to restore communication for individuals who have lost the ability to speak following laryngectomy or radiation-induced damage to the speech articulators after cancer treatment. Beyond assistive communication, these interfaces have broader applications in human-computer interaction, as demonstrated by emerging technologies such as Meta's EMG wristband.

I also study how humans coordinate highly dexterous tasks using multimodal measurements such as EMG, eye gaze, and egocentric video, with the goal of translating principles of human sensorimotor coordination into more capable robotic systems.

A central goal of my research is to make neuromotor interfaces robust and intuitive enough for real-world use. To this end, I study the underlying properties of neuromotor signals and develop algorithms that generalize across populations and operate efficiently on embedded devices in natural environments. My work takes a principled machine-learning approach, with foundational contributions to multivariate time-series learning across biosignals, speech, and multimodal interaction with the physical world. At UC Davis, I have conducted large-scale human-subject studies and built neuromotor interfaces end to end, spanning experimental design and data collection through algorithm development and deployment with real users.
