Research

My research builds neuromotor interfaces that decode human intent directly from multimodal biosignals, with applications spanning assistive communication, human-computer interaction, and robot learning. On the communication side, I develop non-invasive interfaces that restore speech for individuals who have lost the ability to speak following laryngectomy or radiation-induced damage to the speech articulators after cancer treatment; the same underlying technology extends to broader human-computer interaction, as seen in emerging products such as Meta's EMG wristband. I also study how humans coordinate highly dexterous tasks using multimodal measurements such as EMG (electromyography), eye gaze, and egocentric video, to translate principles of human sensorimotor coordination into more capable robots.

A central goal of my research is to make neuromotor interfaces robust and intuitive enough for real-world use. To this end, I study the underlying properties of neuromotor signals and develop algorithms that generalize across populations and operate efficiently on embedded devices in natural environments. My work takes a principled machine-learning approach, with foundational contributions to multivariate time-series learning for biosignals, speech and audio modeling, and multimodal models for interaction with the physical world. At UC Davis, I have conducted large-scale human-subject studies and built neuromotor interfaces end to end, spanning experimental design and data collection through algorithm development and deployment with real users.

EMG-Based Hand-Gesture Interfaces

> 90 participants

This work marked my first investigation into EMG as a modality for human-computer interaction. EMG signals recorded at the wrist can enable interfaces for hand-gesture recognition, keyboard typing, and handwriting recognition. A fundamental challenge, however, is the substantial variability of these signals across individuals. In this project, I asked whether EMG possesses an inherent structure that could serve as an inductive bias for machine-learning algorithms. This question was motivated by the success of structured signal representations in other domains, particularly spectrotemporal representations that have been fundamental to speech and audio modeling.

To investigate this question, I conducted a large-scale study (> 90 subjects), the largest of its kind at the time, across a demographically diverse population that included individuals with high BMI (body-mass index) and adults over 60 years. I found that when EMG signals are represented through covariance matrices, they exhibit a consistent geometric structure that is sufficiently informative to decode discrete gestures even without supervision. Remarkably, this structure persists across individuals and remains largely unaffected by factors such as subcutaneous fat, skin hydration, and age. This work established my foundational understanding of neuromuscular signals and provided the basis for my subsequent work on more complex EMG-based speech interfaces.

EMG-Based Speech Interfaces

> 40 hours data

This project is my absolute favorite. I had the incredible opportunity to build it from the ground up, from collecting and open-sourcing some of the largest speech-EMG datasets to building working EMG-to-text and EMG-to-audio systems. So far, I have collected and open-sourced more than 40 hours of speech EMG from over 15 individuals.

EMG-based speech interfaces have been explored for over a decade, but they had never really scaled to general English, nor was there a clear way to deal with the low-data and highly variable nature of EMG. So, I took a step back and started with the basics: what information about speech is actually present in orofacial EMG? I first showed that EMG can decode discrete orofacial movements, all English phonemes, and words spanning the entire English phoneme repertoire.

Drawing from my earlier work on wrist EMG, I then asked whether orofacial EMG exhibits a similar geometric structure. It does. I went further and developed a simple linear-algebraic explanation for this geometry: EMG from an individual or recording session can be approximately represented using a set of orthogonal basis vectors, and these bases change across individuals and sessions. In other words, a large part of the distribution shift we observe in EMG can be understood as a change of basis. This mechanistic explanation remains one of my favorite results from the project.

I then used this geometric structure to demonstrate direct EMG-to-text decoding. This was perhaps the most elegant result from the project: covariance matrices provide such a strong inductive prior that the relevant spatial structure of EMG is already captured in the representation. As a result, even a purely temporal model such as a vanilla GRU can map continuous EMG directly to phoneme sequences, which can then be composed into words. The same does not hold for conventional EMG representations such as spectrograms, where a GRU alone fails to effectively model the signal. To me, this result was a particularly clean demonstration of why understanding the underlying geometry of EMG matters.

I later extended this work to direct EMG-to-audio synthesis. I am now continuing this project at a much larger scale, conducting longitudinal studies and collecting hundreds of hours of EMG data across months. My goal is to use these data to build foundation models for EMG and translate them into real-time communication systems for individuals who have undergone laryngectomy.

Understanding Human Dexterity

This is my most daring and ambitious project, both in its complexity and scale. Here, I wanted to study human sensorimotor coordination in its full complexity: simultaneously capturing multiple aspects of human behavior, synchronizing heterogeneous sensing modalities, collecting data across a large cohort, and doing so during rich, long-horizon tasks that demand very different forms of dexterity.

To make this possible, I built a multimodal data-collection system from scratch that synchronously records EMG, eye gaze, and egocentric and allocentric video. I have used this system to collect data from a large cohort performing tasks ranging from assembling intricate and complex LEGO structures and cutting and peeling vegetables to in-hand object manipulation and high-torque tool use.

Using these data, I am studying how humans coordinate sensing and movement during complex real-world behavior, with the goal of identifying principles of human sensorimotor coordination that can be translated into robotic systems to improve their dexterity, adaptability, and performance.
